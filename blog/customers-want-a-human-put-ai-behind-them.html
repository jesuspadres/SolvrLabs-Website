Customers Want a Human. Put AI Behind Them.

New surveys say most homeowners would rather talk to a person. With hiring this hard, here's how to keep people up front and let AI do the rest.

Your customers just told you who they want on the phone

A few weeks ago The ACHR NEWS published a survey that every shop owner thinking about AI should read. They asked 782 homeowners which AI uses they'd be comfortable with. Only 36% were OK with AI scheduling appointments or answering basic questions. 24% weren't comfortable with any of the uses on the list. The top worry, at 36%, was losing human judgment and expertise (The ACHR NEWS).

Another survey this summer went further. 87% of 6,000 adults said they'd rather talk to a real person than an AI when they contact a local home service, and 62% don't trust AI to take the right action in an emergency (C&R Magazine). That one was paid for by a live answering service, so take the exact number with some salt. But it points the same way as the ACHR data.

I build AI phone systems for a living, so you might expect me to argue with this. I'm not going to. I think the customers are mostly right. Where I think owners go wrong is the conclusion they draw from it.

The problem: you can't hire the humans

Here's the other half of the picture. In NFIB's October survey, 36% of small business owners had job openings they couldn't fill, up 4 points in a month. 48% of all owners said they got few or no qualified applicants, which is 86% of the ones actually trying to hire. A quarter of owners called labor quality their single biggest problem (NFIB Jobs Report, NFIB Small Business Economic Trends).

Contractors feel it too. ServiceTitan's 2026 Residential State of the Trades report, built on 1,000 contractors, found labor and overhead costs and the skilled labor shortage at the top of the risk list. 74% called AI an "efficiency engine," but only 25% were using it (Electrical Contractor), and nearly half said they don't trust it (HousingWire).

So customers want a person, and you can't find enough people. If you go all-human, the phone rings out when your one office person is at lunch, on another call, or home for Thanksgiving. If you go all-AI, you're ignoring what most of your customers just said out loud. Neither is the answer.

What the shops getting this right actually do

Superior Plumbing: people first, AI as backup

Superior Plumbing has been in the Atlanta area for 40 years. Last fall they added ServiceTitan's AI virtual agent, which their team named Piper. Their CRM specialist said the goal was for the CSRs to see it "as a force multiplier and not their replacement." Customers who need a person still get one. According to ServiceTitan's write-up, the agent booked 80% of calls when it answered first, with 30% escalated to staff, and 70% when it was the fallback (ServiceTitan).

The part I like best is where they used it. During a cold spell, they closed early and sent a CSR home, and the agent took five leads in two hours and converted four. On Thanksgiving and Christmas, no CSR had to work for the first time in 40 years, with a manager on call for escalations. That's AI covering the hours nobody wants to staff, not AI replacing the people customers like talking to.

Southern Home Services: offer both and let the customer pick

Southern Home Services runs about 26 brands across the South. Calls go to the local branch first, roll over to a central call center when the branch is busy, and AI voice agents pick up the overflow and after-hours calls. They reported a nearly 13% year-over-year increase in their call booking rate (ServiceTitan). Jarrod Brinker of Southern Home Services told The ACHR NEWS: "Our job is to offer both options and let the customer decide how they want to engage with us."

Both of these are vendor case studies, so they're the best-case version. And both are bigger than most of the shops I work with. But the pattern scales down fine: a person answers when a person can, and AI catches what would otherwise go to voicemail.

What's real and what's still hype

Real: AI can answer, book and dispatch on its own, and it does well on routine calls like scheduling a tune-up or answering "do you service my area?" It can also work quietly next to a human on a live call, pulling out the details and doing the data entry. That second use gets a lot less attention, and I think it's the one most small shops should start with.

Still hard: the judgment calls. The ACHR survey's top homeowner worry was losing human expertise, and 62% in the other survey don't trust AI in an emergency. A good system knows when to hand off. 61% of people say they get frustrated explaining their problem to an AI before getting transferred to a human (C&R Magazine), so the handoff has to carry the details over. And 85% in that survey say businesses should clearly disclose when you're talking to AI, which is worth thinking about when you set up your greeting.

Hype: that you can switch a bot on, fire the office, and customers won't notice. They notice. The ACHR contractors who use AI were split too: 36% saw better efficiency, but 23% saw no noticeable impact at all.

What to do this week

  • Count what's falling through. Pull last month's missed and after-hours calls from your phone provider. That's the slice AI should cover first.
  • Keep your best people on the calls that need judgment. Emergencies, upset customers, big-ticket estimates.
  • Decide your handoff rules before you turn anything on. What gets transferred live, who's on call, and what gets texted to whom.
  • Plan the holidays now. Write down who answers on Thanksgiving, the day after, and Christmas Eve. If the answer is "voicemail," fix that first.
  • Give customers the choice. If someone wants a person, make it easy to get one.
